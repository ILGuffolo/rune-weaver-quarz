By the morning of the ninth day, Mila knew fourteen words.

She'd written them on the inside cover of a small notebook she'd found under the kitchen table—abandoned, mostly blank, smelling faintly of something between pepper and soap. She hadn't told Kael about the notebook. She hadn't told him she'd also memorized the phonetic list he'd left her, or that she'd spent the better part of three mornings repeating numbers under her breath until the sounds stopped feeling like gravel in her mouth.

She hadn't told him any of it because telling him would mean admitting the words were useful. And she wasn't ready to admit that yet.

Breakfast was a quiet ceasefire.

Kael cooked something hot with eggs in it—she still didn't know the word for eggs—and set a portion in front of her without being asked. She ate it without commenting. He drank something dark and bitter from a clay cup and read through a stack of documents, muttering occasionally to himself. At some point he made an annoyed sound and drew a line through three pages at once.

"Bad news?" Mila asked.

He glanced up. Then he drew a face on the margin of the page—round circle, two dots for eyes, a dramatic frown—and pointed at it.

She snorted before she could stop herself.

He went back to his documents, but with a smile on his face.

The assignment happened after breakfast.

Kael cleared one end of the kitchen table, which was significant enough on its own to make Mila sit up straighter. He sat down a neat stack of sealed papers, each folded and marked with different stamps, and then he pulled out the notebook. His notebook. He had one too, she'd noticed—the sketching one, thick with drawings and symbols, worn at the corners.

He drew a map.

It was actually a decent map, she had to admit. Streets rendered in quick confident lines, a few landmarks labeled in letters she was still learning to parse, small symbols marking three or four different buildings.

He pointed at the sealed papers. Then at the map. Then at the buildings on the map. Then at the papers again.

_Deliver these. Here, here, and here._

"Okay," Mila said. "I'm a mailman."

He didn't understand the word but something in her tone made him pause. He pointed at her, then drew a small figure walking on the map.

"Yeah, I got it." She reached for the papers.

He held up a hand. Drew something else. A small circle with a number inside it.

She squinted. That was... that was one of the numbers she knew. _Xevi_. Seven.

She looked at him.

He pointed at the papers, then at the sun through the window, then made an arc with his hand—morning to midday—and held up two fingers.

Time was measured differently there, there were _Chut, Fiushte, Laschd_ equivalents of seconds minutes and hours. Except seconds where much slower than _Chut_ and a _Fiushte_ was 20 _Chut_. hours were stranger becose they varied in length so you almost always had one or two entire _Laschd_ for every phase of the day. 

"soo... i got two... _Lashd_ ... from brekfast to midday? about three, maybe four hours?"

"That's plenty of time," Mila said confidently, even though she had absolutely no idea how far any of these places were.

She took the papers anyway and stood up.

He handed her one more thing—a small folded card with a sequence of symbols on it. She recognized one of them from the number list. She did not recognize the others.

"What's this?"

He pointed at the papers, then at the card, then mimed handing something over and receiving something back.

A receipt. A signature. Or something like that.

"Fine." She tucked it into her pocket, picked up the map, and headed for the door.

He said something behind her. One word, short, that she didn't know yet.

She looked back.

He made a gesture she'd learned three days ago. _Careful_.

"I know," she said.

The city hit her immediately.

Not the smell—she was half-used to that, wood and something mineral and whatever they burned in the cook-fires by midmorning—but the *sound*. On the handful of three-minute escapes she'd taken since the keycard incident, she'd only ever stood on Kael's threshold and watched. Now she was in it.

The town was not quiet.

The market street—she assumed it was the market street because it was wider and louder and smelled like a dozen different things at once—was already in full swing. Stalls made of wood and canvas stretched in both directions, vendors calling out in their weird language . Someone was frying something that smelled extraordinary similar to chicken but was at least double the size. Someone else was arguing with volume and hand gestures so exaggerated that Mila felt she could almost understand it.

Mila stood at the edge of it all and checked her map.

The first address was... she turned the map, confused. Turned it again.

_That way, probably._

She walked.

The buildings were close together and tall, second and third stories leaning slightly toward each other over the street like they were sharing a secret. There were no numbers on them. 

"This was information I probably should have asked about before leaving."

She stopped a woman carrying a basket and tried. "Sorry—" no, that wasn't the word, she didn't know the word for sorry, she searched her fourteen and found only numbers and household objects—"um. _Thir_."

Door. She'd said *door*. She held up the map. Pointed at the building symbol.

The woman looked at the map. Looked at Mila. Said something slowly.

Mila caught two words she hoped to have recognized.

"Okay," Mila said. "Left and past something. Great."

The woman waited politely in case Mila had more words.

She didn't.

"Thank you," Mila said in English, which the woman didn't understand, and walked away.

The first delivery took forty minutes.

The building was there, after a bit of looking around aimlessly. A low stone structure with a carved symbol above the door that matched the stamp on the paper—she should have looked for that from the start, obvious in retrospect. She walked in. Said the number seven in the local language very confidently—the only number she was certain she could produce without embarrassing herself—and held out the paper and the receipt card.

The official at the desk took the paper. Looked at it. Signed the card. Handed it back.

That was it.

Mila walked back out into the street, receipt in hand, and tried very hard not to feel disproportionately pleased with herself.

She didn't find the second building for another twenty minutes, because she went the wrong direction and ended up at the city's outer wall. She stood with her back against the wall and looked up at the wooden ramparts and felt, for a brief moment, how absurdly far from home she was.

In a strange medieval city, probably on another planet. _Delivering mail_ 

Then she looked at the map and found herself again.

The second building was a tavern.

This was not what she'd expected but the stamp matched the symbol on the paper so she went in anyway. The smell inside was extraordinary—spiced something, bread, old wood, something sweet underneath. A man behind the counter barely looked up at her.

She held out the packet.

He took it. Read it for a moment. Said something she didn't understand.

She held up the receipt card.

He made a face like this was not how this worked. He took the card anyway and handed her something back—a small sealed envelope. Heavier than she expected.

_A reply._

Oh. So these aren't just deliveries. Some of them are _exchanges_.

She put the envelope in her pocket alongside the receipt and walked back out into the sunlight.

The market, on her way to the third address, was the problem.

A woman selling fabric at a stall where someone was demonstrating the colors using—

Using light itself.

A soft, warm, white light, produced from the woman's palm like it cost her nothing, hovering just above the cloth so that the dyes showed true rather than dulled by shadow. She moved it around as naturally as someone adjusting a lamp. The fabric merchant didn't even look at it. Her customer leaned in to examine the stitching.

The vendor noticed her staring and said something. Her customer looked over too. Mila was aware, she was staring at a light spell like she'd never seen anything so incredible.

She hadn't—well, since the two hunters— it felt like forever ago and yesterday at the same time...

She made herself look away and kept walking.

Two stalls later, a kid of about eight flicked a small flame from their fingertip to light a candle, decided the candle was wrong, extinguished it, moved it, tried again. The whole process took about fifteen seconds and the kid's expression throughout was one of total mundane concentration.

She couldn't find the third address.

She walked the entire block twice. She asked two more people, got two different answers, followed both of them, and ended up in a small courtyard where a cat the size of a medium dog watched her with absolute contempt.

She sat down on a low wall.

The cat did not move.

"Don't," Mila told it.

The cat blinked slowly.

She looked at the papers. The third one had a different kind of stamp, heavier, pressed deep into the wax. She turned it over. On the back, in small cramped letters, was a symbol she'd seen on Kael's door once—a routing mark, maybe. Not a street address.

This one wasn't a city office.

This one went back to Kael.

She stared at it.

Then she looked at the third building on the map, which she now noticed was marked slightly differently from the others—a small line through the symbol, which she had completely ignored because she didn't know what lines through symbols meant yet.

She had spent forty minutes looking for an office that didn't need to be found.

She stuffed the paper back in the stack and stood up.

The cat looked deeply unsurprised by her entire situation.

Then she found the right stall, three rows over, and bought what she was actually supposed to buy—a small wrapped parcel of something that smelled like spice and wood.

She made it back before the midday sun hit the top meridian.

By maybe four minutes.

Kael was at his desk when she came in, doing three things at once in the way that seemed to be his natural state. He looked up. She set the papers and receipts and the envelope on the corner of his desk and the parcel on the kitchen table and stood back.

He looked at the receipts. Checked them against something. Picked up the sealed envelope. Set it aside with deliberate care, which suggested it was important. Went through the stack and found the third paper still undelivered.

She had exactly one sentence in the local language available to her for this situation.

"_Rekka thir_?" The second door. Which was the closest she could get to *it goes back to you actually*.

He looked at her.

She held up two fingers—two deliveries done—and pointed at the third paper and then at him.

He made a face that suggested he understood and that this had happened before with different people and he was tired of it but not, specifically, at her.

He set the paper in a different pile.

She waited for the lecture that wasn't coming.

He picked up the parcel from the table, checked it, set it down. Looked at her.

Then he did something she hadn't expected.

He reached into his desk drawer and set two small coins on the edge of the table. Pushed them toward her side with one finger.

She looked at them.

They weren't much. But they were hers. For work she'd done, badly but done.

She picked up the coins.

They were heavier than they looked.

She said nothing.

He was already back to his documents.

She went to her nook, sat on the sofa, and pulled out her notebook. Added three new words she'd learned from her scaring trip into this new world: the word for left. The word for past. Something that might have meant *foreigner* or might have meant *lost*, she was still deciding.
